## 1. General Introduction

### 1.1 Founding and Development

Carnegie Mellon University began in 1900 as the Carnegie Technical Schools, founded by steel industrialist Andrew Carnegie to provide vocational education for the children of workers in Pittsburgh.

In 1912, it became the Carnegie Institute of Technology, commonly known as Carnegie Tech, a four-year degree-granting institution. In 1967, it merged with the Mellon Institute of Industrial Research, founded by the Mellon family, to become the present Carnegie Mellon University. Because of this history, traces of both the Carnegie and Mellon families can be found throughout the campus buildings and facilities. The university email domain, andrew.cmu.edu, uses the founder's name. (Andrew Carnegie & Andrew Mellon)

CMU has about 16,000 students in total, including roughly 7,000 undergraduates and 9,000 graduate students. Compared with large public universities, it is relatively small, and one distinctive feature is that graduate students outnumber undergraduates.

For comparison, the University of Pittsburgh has about 30,000 students in total (about 10,000 undergraduates), while Texas A&M University has about 77,000 students (about 60,000 undergraduates).

### 1.2 Academic Character

CMU is a university that actively promotes interdisciplinary research. It does not have a medical school, law school, dental school, pharmacy school, nursing school, agriculture school, or education school.

Dietrich College of Humanities and Social Sciences includes not only traditional humanities and social science fields but also programs related to statistics and data science. In English, the university is highly regarded for technical writing and rhetoric, especially the study of communicating technical content clearly to general audiences.

The Department of Philosophy studies logic and computability, as well as issues related to artificial intelligence judgment and decision-making. Linguistics also focuses on how machines can understand human language. Within the School of Computer Science, the Language Technologies Institute is established as a separate institute.

### 1.3 Notable Alumni and Figures

James Gosling: Received a Ph.D. in computer science from CMU and developed the Java programming language.

John Nash: The mathematician known as the real-life figure behind the film A Beautiful Mind and a Nobel Prize winner in economics. He received both his bachelor's and master's degrees from CMU.

Andy Warhol: A leading figure in Pop Art who graduated from CMU's College of Fine Arts.

Ethan Hawke, Holly Hunter, and Patrick Wilson: Actors from the CMU School of Drama who have been nominated multiple times for Academy Awards and major theater and film awards.

Luis von Ahn: A CMU computer science Ph.D. alumnus who developed CAPTCHA technology and Duolingo.

Scott Fahlman: A CMU professor known as the person who first proposed the :-) emoticon.

### 1.4 Major Academic Fields

CMU's computer science and robotics fields are regarded as among the top in the United States, alongside MIT, Stanford University, and UC Berkeley. The university played an important role in developing artificial intelligence as an independent academic field and is also known for establishing the world's first human-computer interaction program, robotics institute, and machine learning department.

The arts and drama fields are also highly regarded. CMU was the first university in the United States to grant a degree in drama, and together with institutions such as Juilliard, it is considered one of the country's leading drama education programs. It has produced many Tony Award and Academy Award winners and nominees.

The Tepper School of Business is considered among the top programs in the United States in quantitative analysis, operations management, and production management using data and mathematics.

CMU is distinctive in that both engineering and fine arts are highly developed. Civil and environmental engineering focuses not only on traditional construction fields but also on smart infrastructure, digital twins, and interdisciplinary research.

## 2. Business and Computer Science Area

### 2.1 Tepper Quad

Tepper School of Business and Coulter Welcome Center

Tepper Quad was built through a donation by David Tepper, founder of the hedge fund Appaloosa Management and an alumnus of CMU's business school. Tepper received his MBA from CMU in 1982 and donated $67 million for construction of the building.

The previous business school building, Posner Hall, was relatively isolated on the edge of campus. When Tepper Quad was completed in 2018, the business school moved to this location.

The central architectural idea is interconnectedness. Around a large central glass atrium, open glass walls and broad common spaces are arranged so that students in business, engineering, computer science, and the arts can naturally meet and interact.

### 2.2 Gates and Hillman Centers

Gates Center for Computer Science and Hillman Center for Future-Generation Technologies

The Gates and Hillman Centers were completed in 2009, supported by a $20 million donation from the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation and a $10 million donation from Pittsburgh philanthropist Henry Hillman.

The buildings were constructed by using the existing slope of what had originally been a steep valley rather than completely flattening the terrain. The exterior is finished with asymmetrical zinc panels.

Inside is the Helix, a spiral ramp about 630 feet long that connects the third through fifth floors. This space was designed to encourage researchers to encounter one another casually while moving through the building rather than remaining only in their labs.

Facilities related to human-computer interaction research are also located in this area. The ML and HCI departments were first established at CMU.

### 2.3 Randy Pausch Memorial Bridge

Randy Pausch Memorial Bridge

The Randy Pausch Memorial Bridge was built to commemorate the late Randy Pausch, a professor in CMU's computer science department.

After being diagnosed with terminal pancreatic cancer, Randy Pausch gave The Last Lecture, about childhood dreams and lessons from life. The lecture later became known worldwide.

The bridge connects Gates and Hillman Centers, the computer science buildings, with Purnell Center for the Arts, the College of Fine Arts building. This symbolizes Randy Pausch's philosophy emphasizing the fusion of technology and art. At night, LED lights installed on the bridge exterior turn on.

The aluminum panels on the bridge railing contain penguin-shaped openings. These symbolize the First Penguin Award, which Randy Pausch gave in his class to the team that attempted the boldest challenge but failed. Like a penguin that risks predators and jumps into the sea first, it honors the courage to attempt something without fear of failure.

One of Randy Pausch's representative messages is:

The walls are not there to stop us. They are there to give us a chance to show how badly we want something.

### 2.4 Purnell Center for the Arts

Purnell Center for the Arts

Purnell Center for the Arts was named after CMU alumnus and donor J. Stanley Purnell and was completed in 1999.

It is used as a major education and performance facility for the CMU School of Drama, and inside it is the Chosky Theater, a circular performance space.

### 2.5 Warner Hall

Warner Hall

Warner Hall was named after J. C. Warner, CMU's fourth president.

J. C. Warner was also a chemist who participated in the Manhattan Project and conducted research on plutonium purification.

## 3. Central Campus and Student Facilities

### 3.1 Walking to the Sky

Walking to the Sky

Walking to the Sky is a 2006 work by sculptor Jonathan Borofsky, an alumnus of CMU's College of Fine Arts.

It depicts people of different genders, races, and ages walking upward toward the sky along a column about 100 feet tall. It symbolizes endless academic inquiry and human potential and serves as a representative landmark of the central campus plaza.

### 3.2 Cohon University Center

Cohon University Center

Cohon University Center was named after Jared Cohon, a civil and environmental engineer who served as CMU's eighth president.

Through a major expansion in 2016, a fitness center with a glass exterior was added. Today, it is a central space of campus life where dining facilities, athletic facilities, meeting rooms, and student support facilities are gathered.

The lower level of the building houses the official CMU bookstore.

## 4. South Athletic Facilities

### 4.1 Highmark Center for Health, Wellness and Athletics

Highmark Center for Health, Wellness and Athletics

The Highmark Center was built with $35 million in support from Highmark Health, a healthcare company headquartered in Pittsburgh, and was completed in 2024.

It is not simply an athletic facility, but an integrated wellness facility that provides physical health, mental health counseling, and medical services in one place.

### 4.2 Gesling Stadium

Gesling Stadium

Gesling Stadium was named after CMU alumnus William Gesling.

It was created using the valley terrain on the south side of campus. Instead of installing large separate stands, it uses the slope of the hill as viewing space, which is a distinctive feature.

## 5. East Core Campus and Arts/Architecture Area

### 5.1 Margaret Morrison Carnegie Hall

Margaret Morrison Carnegie Hall

Margaret Morrison Carnegie Hall was built in 1906 by Andrew Carnegie in honor of his mother, Margaret Morrison.

In its early years, it was used as a vocational school for women. Unlike engineering buildings with many gear and machine decorations, it uses more curved and delicate ornamentation, such as lotus patterns on the entrance ceiling and spinning-wheel motifs.

### 5.2 Posner Hall

Posner Hall

Posner Hall was built through a donation from the Posner family. Until Tepper Quad was completed in 2018, it was used as the business school building.

### 5.3 Kraus Campo

Kraus Campo

Kraus Campo is a rooftop garden of about 100 by 150 feet located on top of Posner Hall. It was designed by artist Mel Bochner.

Campo means plaza or field in Italian. Therefore, Kraus Campo means "Kraus Plaza," named after the donors, the Kraus couple.

The garden has curving paths that look as if they were drawn with a brush, and it is composed as a space using mathematical forms and visual illusion.

### 5.4 College of Fine Arts

College of Fine Arts

The College of Fine Arts building was designed by Henry Hornbostel, the architect who designed CMU's campus master plan in 1912.

The building was designed around the idea of making the building itself an architectural textbook so that American students who could not easily visit Europe could study architectural history.

On the lobby floor, plans of major world buildings such as the Parthenon and St. Peter's Basilica are engraved at actual scale. On the ceiling and walls, architectural styles from several periods, including Renaissance and Gothic, are represented in frescoes.

Pop artist Andy Warhol studied here and received a bachelor's degree in Pictorial Design, a commercial art field, in 1949.

### 5.5 Hunt Library

Hunt Library

Hunt Library was built through a $2.8 million donation by Roy Hunt of the Hunt family, the founding family of the global aluminum company Alcoa, and was completed in 1961.

Symbolizing Pittsburgh's steel and aluminum industries, the entire exterior of the building is covered with silvery aluminum frames. At night, it was designed so that light shines through the spaces between the aluminum panels.

### 5.6 The Fence and The Cut

The Fence and The Cut

The Cut is a broad lawn located at the center of campus, and The Fence is the fence installed at its center.

In the past, there was a bridge on campus where students often gathered, but it was removed in 1916. In 1923, senior students who missed it built a wooden fence on The Cut to create a new meeting place, which is known as the beginning of The Fence.

The early Fence was used as a place where students leaned, rested, or met. Later, after one fraternity secretly painted the Fence at night to promote a party, a tradition formed in which student groups paint the Fence to announce events and messages.

As paint was repeatedly added for decades, the thickness and weight of the Fence increased greatly. It was once listed in Guinness World Records as the world's most painted object. However, because of the weight of the paint, it collapsed in 1989 and 1993, and afterward it was replaced with a structure using a concrete core and wire mesh.

Fence painting rules:

Painting must be done only between midnight and sunrise.

Rollers or spray paint cannot be used; only brushes may be used.

Only part of the Fence may not be painted; the entire Fence must be painted at once.

To preserve the completed message, at least two students must stay beside the Fence overnight to guard it.

## 6. West Engineering Quad

### 6.1 Doherty Hall

Doherty Hall

Doherty Hall was named after Robert Doherty, an electrical engineer who served as CMU's third president. It is one of the early engineering buildings, completed in 1908.

### 6.2 Baker Hall and Porter Hall

Baker Hall and Porter Hall

Baker Hall and Porter Hall were named respectively after Thomas Baker, the first president of Carnegie Tech, and John Porter, a member of the board of trustees.

The long corridor connecting the two buildings is called the Infinite Corridor.

The exterior walls use yellow bricks known as Carnegie Yellow Brick. These bricks are said to have been made so that the black soot from Pittsburgh's old steel mills would be less noticeable.

These buildings were not commercial manufacturing factories. In the early vocational education period, they were used as practice spaces and machine halls where students operated large machines and refined iron.

Walking along the Infinite Corridor, one can notice slight uphill and downhill slopes in the hallway. This is because the buildings were constructed lengthwise along the existing valley terrain without completely removing the slope.

There is also a story that Andrew Carnegie designed the buildings so that they could be converted into factories if the school failed, but this is closer to a campus urban legend than an officially confirmed fact.

### 6.3 Hamerschlag Hall

Hamerschlag Hall

Hamerschlag Hall was named after Arthur Hamerschlag, the first president of Carnegie Tech, appointed directly by Andrew Carnegie.

The top of the building has a Renaissance-style dome. When completed in 1906, it was used as a thermal power plant supplying electricity to the entire campus.

The dome was a structure designed to architecturally conceal the exhaust stack of the power plant. It can be seen as an example of combining the functional elements of an industrial facility with the campus landscape.

### 6.4 Wean Hall

Wean Hall

Wean Hall was built through a donation from industrialist Raymond Wean and was completed in 1971.

It is a representative example of Brutalist architecture, exposing concrete on the exterior without decorative finishing. Its appearance resembles a massive fortress or bunker.

On the first floor is La Prima Espresso, a Pittsburgh-area coffee brand. La Prima has operated since 1988 using Italian-style custom roasting machines and is a space frequently used by students and researchers.

### 6.5 ANSYS Hall

ANSYS Hall

ANSYS Hall was built with support from ANSYS, an engineering simulation software company, and was completed in 2019. ANSYS founder John Swanson is a CMU alumnus. He was originally a Westinghouse engineer but left the company to start his own business in order to develop general-purpose finite element analysis software. ANSYS software is used in various engineering fields, including finite element analysis in nuclear engineering and architectural engineering.

The first floor of the building is used as a large makerspace with a full glass front.

### 6.6 Scaife Hall

Scaife Hall

Scaife Hall was named to commemorate a donation from Pittsburgh's Scaife Foundation.

After a recent full renovation, it is used as a research and manufacturing facility for mechanical engineering and robotics.

## 7. Pittsburgh Landmarks

### 7.1 Phipps Conservatory

Phipps Conservatory

Phipps Conservatory is a large Victorian-style glass greenhouse donated by Henry Phipps, a business partner of Andrew Carnegie.

Below the conservatory is the Panther Hollow valley, and this area connects to Schenley Park, one of Pittsburgh's major large parks.

Schenley Park is about 1.85 square kilometers, about three times the size of CMU's main campus and about three and a half times the size of the University of Pittsburgh's main campus. It is wider than the two university campuses combined.

### 7.2 Schenley Plaza

Schenley Plaza

Schenley Plaza was originally a concrete parking lot that accommodated about 280 cars.

Through an urban regeneration project in 2006, it was rebuilt as about five acres of lawn plaza and public resting space.

### 7.3 Carnegie Museums and Library Complex

Carnegie Museums and Library Complex

The Carnegie Museums and Library Complex was built by Andrew Carnegie in 1895.

Within one building are the Carnegie Museum of Natural History, Carnegie Museum of Art, Music Hall, and the main branch of the Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh.

The Carnegie Museum of Natural History is considered one of the major natural history museums in the United States and is especially famous for its Jurassic dinosaur fossil collection.

Its representative exhibit, Diplodocus carnegii, is a skeleton composed largely of actual fossils, with missing parts supplemented using other specimens and reconstructions.

## 8. University of Pittsburgh

### 8.1 Hillman Library

Hillman Library

Hillman Library is the central library of the University of Pittsburgh. It was built with support from the Henry Hillman Foundation, which also donated to the construction of CMU's Gates and Hillman Centers, and was completed in 1968.

### 8.2 Heinz Memorial Chapel

Heinz Memorial Chapel

Heinz Memorial Chapel was built by the children of H. J. Heinz, founder of the Heinz Company, in his memory, and was completed in 1938.

It was designed in the French Gothic Revival style, and inside are 23 large stained-glass windows, some reaching about 73 feet in height.

### 8.3 Cathedral of Learning

Cathedral of Learning

The Cathedral of Learning was an architectural project promoted in 1926 by John G. Bowman, then chancellor of the University of Pittsburgh.

It was planned as a 42-story building to accommodate the rapidly increasing number of students while visually expressing the dignity of learning. During the Great Depression, construction proceeded through fundraising by students and Pittsburgh citizens.

The building is about 535 feet tall and is known as the second-tallest university building in the world.

The first-floor Commons Room covers about 0.5 acres and was built with Indiana limestone. It is a space designed so that stone and intersecting vaulted arches distribute the load without exposed structural steel.

Nationality Rooms

On the first and third floors of the Cathedral of Learning are the Nationality Rooms, which commemorate the cultures of various immigrant communities that settled in Pittsburgh.

Each classroom recreates the traditional architecture and interior decoration of its country. A distinctive feature is that these are not museum exhibition rooms but actual classrooms. Of the 31 rooms, 29 are used for classes.

Korean Heritage Room

The Korean Heritage Room is in Room 324 on the third floor of the Cathedral of Learning.

It was created with motifs from 14th-century Goryeo-period architectural style and Myeongnyundang at Sungkyunkwan.

### 8.4 William Pitt Union

William Pitt Union

William Pitt Union was originally Hotel Schenley, a luxury hotel that opened in 1898.

Andrew Carnegie, Henry Frick, President Theodore Roosevelt, and baseball player Babe Ruth were among those who used this representative social facility of Pittsburgh.

In 1956, the University of Pittsburgh purchased the building and converted it into a student union.
